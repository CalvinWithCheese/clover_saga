Before her name ever touched the road, before steel ever rang in her hands, Clover Stonefield was already certain of one thing:
Her name was going to be remembered.

The fields of Rorikstead stretched wide and patient under the sun, golden and unbothered, whispering in the wind with a kind of stubborn calm that had no interest in legend. The earth cared about planting and harvest, about rain that came when it pleased and stones that never moved unless someone put their back into it. It did not care for glory, and it certainly did not care for a loud, dirt-streaked girl who swung sticks like swords and declared victories over enemies that did not exist.
Clover ignored this completely.
She charged across those fields as if they were battlefields waiting to be properly named, her boots kicking up dry dust, her hair half-tangled and sunlit, her voice already raised in narration long before any imaginary foe had been struck down.
“Did you see that one?” she shouted, breathless and triumphant, though there had been no witness but the wind. “That one nearly took my arm off! You would have lost your head clean if I hadn’t stepped in!”
Erik, seated on the low fence where he had been pretending to read, lowered his book just enough to watch her finish her performance. His expression carried the careful patience of someone who had long ago accepted that Clover would always be in the middle of something loud.
“I didn’t see it,” he said, because he always answered honestly.
Clover skidded to a stop in front of him, scandalized by the very idea.
“You didn’t see it?” she demanded, hands already describing the size of the imaginary opponent. “It was enormous. Bigger than a cart horse. It had claws like—like this—” she flexed her fingers in front of his face, nearly poking him in the eye “—and it came right at me, and I went straight for it, obviously, because what else would I do—”
“Run,” Erik offered mildly.
She laughed at him, loud and delighted, as if he had just told the finest joke in all of Skyrim.
“Run?” she echoed. “From that? I would never run. Not from anything worth fighting.”
This was said with such complete certainty that it hung in the air like a banner already nailed into place.
Erik watched her for a moment longer, then closed his book around a finger to hold his place.
“You didn’t even bring a sword,” he pointed out.
Clover looked down at the stick in her hand as though it had briefly forgotten its purpose, then lifted it again with renewed conviction.
“This is a sword,” she said firmly. “It’s just not made of iron yet.”
There was, in this, no trace of pretending. The difference between what was and what would be existed only as a matter of time, and time, to Clover, was something that moved in her direction.
She climbed up beside him on the fence without invitation, still breathing hard, still glowing with the afterheat of a battle that had taken place entirely inside her own head.
“They’ll tell stories about it,” she went on, already refining the tale. “Not this one, I mean. A better one. A real one. But like this. Bigger. Properly dangerous.”
Her hands moved as she spoke, carving shapes out of the air, building a future that felt as solid to her as the wood beneath them.
“About how I fought something no one else would touch,” she continued, her voice lowering just enough to suggest importance rather than secrecy. “And I didn’t back down, not once. And everyone will know my name because of it.”
Erik studied her, not with doubt, but with a kind of quiet attention, as if he were trying to understand how a person could believe something so completely before it had ever happened.
“And then what?” he asked.
Clover blinked at him, as though the question itself were strange.
“What do you mean, then what?” she said. “Then I keep doing it.”
He almost smiled at that.
The wind moved through the fields again, bending the grasses in long, rolling waves, the kind that could swallow a quieter person whole. Clover sat in the middle of it like a stone that refused to be smoothed down, bright and unyielding and already speaking over the world rather than listening to it.
Somewhere, far beyond the fields, there were halls where warriors gathered, where names were spoken with weight and remembered long after the voices that first said them had gone quiet. Somewhere, there were battles that did not vanish the moment they ended, and enemies that struck back harder than anything Clover had yet imagined.
She did not yet know the shape of those things.
But she knew, with a certainty that did not ask permission from experience or caution or sense, that she belonged among them.
And if the world had not yet noticed, that was only because she had not reached it.
Not yet.

If the fields did not agree with Clover’s destiny, the stories did.
They came to her the way rain came to the plains—sometimes in sudden bursts, sometimes in long, steady stretches, soaking deep into her until there was no part of her untouched by them. She collected them greedily, with the same wholehearted appetite she brought to everything else, taking them from passing travelers, from old farmhands who pretended not to remember as much as they did, from merchants who could be coaxed into talking if one listened long enough and loudly enough.
Clover did both.
She would plant herself wherever a story had begun to stir—on a crate, on a fence, on the ground if she had to—and lean forward as if proximity alone might make the tale sharper.
“And then what?” she would press, before the speaker had even finished a sentence. “No, no, wait—did he swing first, or did the other one? You’re skipping the best part—go back—what did it sound like?”
She demanded detail the way other people demanded food, and she devoured it just as quickly.
The great heroes of old did not remain distant figures to her. They took on weight, motion, heat. She could see them, hear them, feel the impact of their blows in her own bones as if she had been standing there beside them all along. When the stories reached their crescendos, she would grin so wide it bordered on feral delight, her hands tightening unconsciously, her shoulders squaring as if preparing to step into the fight herself.
And always, sooner or later, the stories circled back to the same place.
Jorrvaskr.
The Companions.
She loved those stories best.
Not just for the battles, though those were glorious enough, but for the way they gathered people together. Warriors who were not solitary figures wandering alone into legend, but a company—men and women bound by shared purpose, by skill, by recognition. A hall where strength was seen and named, where deeds were not swallowed by the wind but spoken aloud, again and again, until they settled into something lasting.
A place where a name could take root and grow.
“They all sit there,” Clover said once, pacing in front of Erik as she retold a story she had heard only an hour before, already expanding it as she went. “All of them. Eating, talking, arguing about who fought better and who nearly died stupidly, and everyone listening because it matters.”
She stopped, turned, and jabbed a finger toward the vague east as if Jorrvaskr might appear if she pointed hard enough.
“That’s where you go if you’re serious,” she declared. “That’s where the real ones are.”
Erik, seated in the shade with his book open again, glanced up at her.
“You’ve never been,” he said.
“I don’t have to have been,” Clover shot back immediately. “I know what it is.”
And she did, in the way that mattered to her.
Not in detail, not in truth that had been tested by experience, but in shape and meaning and feeling. To Clover, the Companions were not simply a group of warriors in a hall. They were the proof that what she wanted was real, that there was a place in the world where a person could become the thing she had already decided she was meant to be.
It did not make her despise the life she had.
Far from it.
Clover worked.
She worked because work was what there was to do, and because her body answered to it naturally. She lifted, hauled, dug, carried, drove her strength into the ground and pulled results back out of it with a kind of stubborn satisfaction. There was no part of her that shrank from effort. When something was heavy, she leaned into it. When something resisted, she pushed harder.
She took a straightforward pride in that.
There was a rightness to a day spent in honest labor, to sweat earned and muscles spent, to seeing the mark of her own effort laid plainly across the land. She did not turn her nose up at it, did not speak of it as lesser or beneath her.
It simply was not the end of her story.
The fields could shape her, but they could not contain her. Every furrow she walked only made the horizon more visible, and every story she heard stretched that horizon farther still.
There were bigger things than harvest.
There were louder things than wind.
There were moments that did not pass quietly back into the earth, but struck and stayed and were carried forward in voices that did not forget.
Clover wanted that.
Not someday, not vaguely, not as a distant hope she might grow into if circumstances allowed.
She wanted it with the same immediacy she brought to everything else, as if the only reason it had not yet happened was that she had not stood up and started walking toward it.
The thought settled into her not as a question, but as a realization that had somehow been waiting for her to catch up.
She stopped in the middle of the yard, hands still dusty from work, breath steady from effort, and turned toward Erik with sudden, blazing clarity.
“That’s it,” she said.
He looked up, already halfway through marking his place.
“That’s what?” he asked.
“We’re going,” Clover said, as if the matter had been under discussion for some time and was now finally resolved. “To Whiterun. To Jorrvaskr. I’m going to join the Companions and become a proper, actual hero, not just the kind people should be telling stories about but aren’t yet.”
She crossed the distance between them in a few long strides, her energy already shifting from decision to motion.
“We’ve waited long enough,” she went on, entirely certain of this despite having never once considered waiting at all. “There’s no reason to stay here when that’s out there.”
She jerked her chin toward the road, toward the world beyond the fields, toward everything she had already decided belonged to her.
Erik regarded her for a moment, the corner of his mouth lifting in something quiet and knowing.
“You’ve decided,” he said.
“Of course I have,” Clover replied, as if there were no other possible way such a thing could happen. “Come on. We’ll leave in the morning.”
She said it without asking, without pausing, without even imagining that he might not be there when she started walking.
For Clover, some things were simply given – the most of which was, of course, was that her name was going to be remembered.
